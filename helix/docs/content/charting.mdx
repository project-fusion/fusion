# Charting

Helix has these chart families, and each one has its own API page:

- `Bar Chart`
- `Spatial Bar Chart`, drawn in three dimensions
- `Line Chart`
- `Spatial Ribbon Chart`, drawn in three dimensions
- `Area Chart`
- `Scatter Chart`
- `Spatial Scatter Chart`, drawn in three dimensions
- `Spatial Surface Chart`, drawn in three dimensions
- `Donut Chart`
- `Spatial Treemap`, drawn in three dimensions
- `Heatmap`
- `Spatial Heatmap`, drawn in three dimensions
- `Sparkline`
- `Trend`
- `Legend`, which all of the charts share

This guide covers which chart fits which question, and how data gets into a
chart. Data gets in the same way for every chart.

## Choose a chart

Choose a chart by the question it answers.

- **How much, compared.** `Bar Chart`. A bar's length is its value, so it always
  starts at zero.
- **How much, across two sets of names.** `Spatial Bar Chart`, with a bar for
  each category and series, standing in a box you can turn. Use it where the
  shape across both matters more than any one value. `Bar Chart` is read more
  exactly.
- **How something moved over time.** `Line Chart`. It measures from the data's
  own range, so a small change still shows. Its `area` puts a light wash under
  each line. Each wash is drawn from the floor on its own and overlaps the
  others.
- **How several series moved over the same places, each apart.** `Spatial
  Ribbon Chart`, with a ribbon for each series, one behind another, in a box
  you can turn. Use it where series cross and a line chart tangles them. `Line
  Chart` is read more exactly.
- **How a total is made up over time.** `Area Chart`. Each column is stacked on
  the one below, so the top edge is the total and a band's thickness is its
  share. Use it when the parts add up to a total. `Line Chart` with `area`
  overlaps the series, and `Area Chart` adds them.
- **Parts of one whole, at one moment.** `Donut Chart`.
- **How large each item is within its group, and how it stands on a second
  measure.** `Spatial Treemap`, with a block for each row, its area its size
  and its height the second measure, gathered by group on a floor you can
  turn. Use it where many items share a whole, such as each service's
  requests and its response time. Its table and its tooltip carry the exact
  values.
- **Whether two measures relate.** `Scatter Chart`, with one point per row.
- **Where rows fall on three measures, and which gather together.** `Spatial
  Scatter Chart`, with one point per row in a box you can turn. Use it for data
  that has three dimensions, such as clusters or embeddings. Its table and its
  tooltip carry the exact values.
- **How one measure rises and falls over two others.** `Spatial Surface Chart`,
  with one row per place on a grid, drawn as a surface you can turn. Use it for
  a measure over two ranges, such as load by hour and by day. Its table carries
  the exact values.
- **How each day or check went.** `Heatmap`, for uptime, contributions, or
  activity.
- **The same days, as a relief.** `Spatial Heatmap` reads the same table as
  `Heatmap` and draws each reading as a prism as tall as its value. Use it where
  the overall shape matters more than any one exact value, such as a busy
  stretch in a year of commits. The tooltip and the table carry the exact
  values.
- **The shape of a series beside its number.** `Sparkline`, drawn with no axes.
  Use `Trend` to show which way one measurement moved and whether that is good
  news.

The six spatial charts draw themselves in 3D in SVG, which a
print and a page without WebGL show too, and a drag or Shift with an arrow
turns. `@fusion.dev/helix-plot` can also draw all six with three.js, lit and
in perspective, in a view you can drag round and zoom. The
[spatial charts guide](./spatial-charts) covers both.

## Charts read their data from a table

```html
<table id="revenue">
  <caption>Revenue by quarter</caption>
  <thead>
    <tr><th>Quarter</th><th>Direct</th><th>Partner</th></tr>
  </thead>
  <tbody>
    <tr><th>Q1</th><td>120</td><td>80</td></tr>
    <tr><th>Q2</th><td>150</td><td>95</td></tr>
  </tbody>
</table>

<helix-bar-chart for="revenue"></helix-bar-chart>
```

`for` points to a real `<table>` by id, and the chart reads its series from that
table. No other Helix component gets its data this way. There are two reasons.

First, a property does not survive hydration. A framework's server render emits
markup, and a property is not markup. An array assigned to `items` exists only
after JavaScript has run, so the server has nothing to send and the first paint
has nothing to draw. Text in the document is the one channel that both the
server and the client can see.

Second, the table is what a screen reader reads. A chart is a picture of
numbers, and a screen reader cannot read a picture. The table holds the numbers
in the document, where you can navigate them, and the chart draws them. If the
table should not be seen, hide it visually, and the data is still there:

```html
<table
  data-hidden-visually
  id="revenue"
>…</table>
```

A table hidden this way takes no room on the page, however many rows it has.
Inside an element with a `transform` or a `filter`, a long hidden table still
lengthens that element's scrolling area. Wrap the table in a hidden `<div>`
there:

```html
<div data-hidden-visually>
  <table id="revenue">…</table>
</div>
```

The table's caption becomes the chart's accessible name unless you set `label`.
The caption usually has the right words already.

Each chart writes a short summary for screen readers, which starts with that
name. The drawing is hidden from screen readers, and the summary gives the one
reading a chart is usually read for:

- `Bar Chart`: each series' highest reading.
- `Line Chart`: each series' latest reading.
- `Area Chart`: the latest total and each part's share of it.
- `Donut Chart`: each slice's share of the whole.
- `Scatter Chart`: each series' number of points and how strongly the two
  measures relate.
- `Spatial Bar Chart`: its number of bars, series, and categories, and the
  range of its values.
- `Spatial Ribbon Chart`: its number of readings, series, and places, and the
  range of its values.
- `Spatial Scatter Chart`: its three measures and its number of points.
- `Spatial Surface Chart`: its measures, its number of readings, and the range
  of its heights.
- `Spatial Treemap`: its number of items and groups, and the range of their
  sizes.

`Heatmap` and `Spatial Heatmap` are each an image with that name. `Sparkline` takes a `label` of its own.

## Tables that change

A chart follows its table. It redraws when you edit a cell, add a row, or
replace the table with a new one under the same id. It also draws a table that
arrives after the chart does, such as one a framework renders once its data
loads.

A blank cell, or one that does not hold a number, is a gap in its series. The
row keeps its place on the axis, a line breaks at the gap, a bar chart draws no
bar there, and a heatmap draws the cell with no tone. The readings after the gap
stay under their own labels, and the tooltip for that row still shows the
row's cells.

A cell holds a number when its whole text reads as one, such as `1200` or
`-3.5`. A formatted number, such as `1,200` or `$1.2k`, is a gap. To chart a
formatted cell, write its number in `data-value`:

```html
<tr><th>Q1</th><td data-value="1200">$1.2k</td><td data-value="800">$800</td></tr>
```

The chart reads `data-value` when a cell has one, and the cell's text otherwise.
The table and the tooltip still show `$1.2k`.

## Charts with many readings

A chart builds what it can show, and that is bounded by its size. The readings
themselves are read again each time the table or the data changes.

- **Names on the axis.** `Line Chart`, `Area Chart`, and `Bar Chart` draw every
  reading's name where every name fits. Where they do not, the chart draws a
  regular share of them, and always the first and the last. Where every row
  header holds a `<time>`, the names drawn start a period, such as the first
  reading of each month or each year. Where the names are numbers in order,
  such as years, they are round numbers. Other names are drawn every so many
  readings, counted from the first. The chart works out the share again when it
  is resized. The tooltip and the keys still say each reading's own name.
- **Gridlines.** `gridlines="category"` rules the readings whose names are
  drawn.
- **Lines.** Where a line has more readings than the plot has pixels across, it
  is drawn through the points those pixels can show: in each column of pixels,
  the first reading, the lowest, the highest, and the last.
- **Markers.** `markers` marks every reading, with one path for each series.
- **Columns.** `Bar Chart` draws each column as an element while every column
  and the space between two categories are at least a pixel wide. Past that, it
  draws each series' columns as one path, each column of pixels as tall as the
  tallest column in it, and draws the category the tooltip describes again over
  them, at least two pixels thick.
- **Points.** `Scatter Chart` draws each series' points as paths. Points that
  overlap are drawn in separate groups, up to four deep, so each keeps its edge
  and a cluster reads darker. The point the keys rest on is drawn again over
  them.
- **The table drawn from `data`.** Past a few hundred readings, the hidden table
  holds one page of them at a time. Its markup gives a screen reader the number
  of rows in the whole table and the place of each row, and the words after it
  say which readings the page holds. Two buttons after it show the previous and
  the next page. You press them with a screen reader as you read. They are left
  out of the tab order, so the plot stays the chart's one tab stop. The keys on
  the plot move through every reading.
- **A table on the page.** A chart reads the whole table that `for` names, and
  the table is part of your page, however many rows it has. Where the readings
  are too many to put in the document, draw the chart from `data`.

## Series colors

Helix has twenty-four series color tokens, in order, from
`--helix-color-series-1` to `--helix-color-series-24`. A chart with three series
uses the first three. The tokens are ordered so that each color
stays distinguishable from the ones before it. They are not designed to look
good as a full set of twenty-four.

The palette wraps after the last color. One number in one place sets where it
wraps, so a chart and the legend beside it always agree on each series' color.

Each mark in a chart carries its series color as a custom property. The chart
tracks which series each bar belongs to, and CSS cannot count that. With the
custom property, you can restyle a series from outside the chart without knowing
the drawing order.

## Add a legend

```html
<helix-legend for="revenue"></helix-legend>
<helix-bar-chart for="revenue"></helix-bar-chart>
```

The legend reads the same table as the chart, so their names and colors always
match. They can differ only if you point them at two different tables.

## Charts on right-to-left pages

On a page with `dir="rtl"`, an axis that follows reading order starts on the
right, where the text starts. Categories and time follow reading order. The
words around a chart are HTML and change direction on their own. The drawing
changes direction with them, so the name under a column belongs to that column
in either direction. The pointer and the tooltip read the drawing the same way.
An axis of values keeps its own direction.

These parts change direction:

- `Bar Chart`'s columns, and its rows, which grow out of their names at the
  right
- `Line Chart`, `Area Chart`, and `Sparkline`, with their end marks
- `Heatmap`'s weeks, which were already a grid laid out along the line
- `Spatial Heatmap`'s weeks in its SVG drawing. A three.js drawing keeps its
  direction, and the chart's labels follow it.
- A half `Donut Chart`, which is read across

These parts keep their direction:

- Values keep their vertical direction, because up means more in every script.
- Numerals are text beside the drawing, so none of them reads backwards.
- A whole `Donut Chart` keeps its first slice at twelve o'clock and runs
  clockwise. That is a clock's convention, and it does not depend on the script.
- `Scatter Chart` is laid out as on a left-to-right page, with its y axis on the
  left and larger values to the right. Both of its axes are measures, and a
  number line runs that way in every script. Its axis names read in their own
  direction, so a name in Arabic reads right to left under the far end of the
  plot. Its summary and tooltip follow the page's direction.
- `Spatial Scatter Chart` and `Spatial Surface Chart` are laid out as on a
  left-to-right page, because all three of their axes are measures. Their
  summaries and tooltips follow the page's direction.
- `Spatial Bar Chart`, `Spatial Ribbon Chart`, and `Spatial Treemap` are laid
  out as on a left-to-right page too, since each drawing is a box you turn. Its summary and tooltip follow the page's
  direction.

To keep a chart left to right on a right-to-left page, such as a number line in
a mathematics text, set `dir="ltr"` on the chart element. You need nothing else.

## Chart animation and reduced motion

Charts animate when they appear and when their data changes. Every chart
responds to `prefers-reduced-motion`. See [Motion](./motion).

## What Helix charts do not include

Helix charts have no axis configuration language, no plugin system, and no chart
types that you build by composing scales. Each chart is a drawing that reads a
table and is styled by the token system, and the table is its accessible
representation. When you need a grammar of graphics, use a charting library.
